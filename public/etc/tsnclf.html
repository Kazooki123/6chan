Thou Shalt Not Commit Logical Fallacies
& Suffer From Cognitive Biases.
Know Thyself before Typing Thy Keyboard.
A Rulebook to NOT Embarrass Thyself.
Every post on @6chan is a fair game for a TSNCLF! callout if it commits any of the sins listed below. This is not a personal attack — it is a gift. Study this page. Refer back often. The internet does not forget, but it WILL forgive if you learn.
TSNCLF!

When someone on @6chan drops this, they're pointing to this page. Find your fallacy. Acknowledge it. Rebuild your argument. That's how this works.

Logical Fallacies
Ad Hominem
argumentum ad hominem
Attacking the person making the argument rather than the argument itself. Their character, appearance, or past is irrelevant to whether they're right.
Example: "Why should I listen to your take on economics? You failed maths in school."
Straw Man
homo stramineus
Misrepresenting someone's argument into a weaker or more extreme version, then attacking that version instead of what they actually said.
Example: "You said we should reduce military spending — so you want us completely defenceless?"
False Dichotomy
bifurcatio
Presenting only two options when more exist. Also called black-and-white thinking or false dilemma. Reality almost always has more than two positions.
Example: "You're either with us or against us."
Slippery Slope
in infinitum
Claiming one small step will inevitably lead to extreme consequences, without demonstrating why each step would necessarily follow.
Example: "If we allow X, the next thing you know we'll have Y, then Z, then total collapse."
Circular Reasoning
circulus in probando
Using your conclusion as a premise of your own argument. The proof just restates the claim in different words.
Example: "This book is true because it says so in the book."
Hasty Generalization
secundum quid
Drawing a broad conclusion from a sample that is too small, unrepresentative, or cherry-picked. Anecdotes are not data.
Example: "I met two people from that city and they were rude. Everyone from there is rude."
Red Herring
ignoratio elenchi
A piece of information / argument that's intentionally or unintentionally false. Used to distract the opponent from the current argument or original issue.
Example: "Why are we focusing about X when Y & Z have it worse!"
Post Hoc
post hoc ergo propter hoc
Assuming that because B happened after A, A caused B. Correlation is not causation. Sequence is not consequence.
Example: "I wore my lucky socks and we won — the socks made us win."
No True Scotsman
no true scotsman
Dismissing counterexamples by redefining the category to exclude them. Moving the goalposts after the fact to protect a generalization.
Example: "No real fan of X would ever do Y — so anyone who did Y wasn't a real fan."
False Cause
non causa pro causa
Incorrectly identifying the cause of something. Related to post hoc but broader — any assumption of causation without sufficient evidence.
Example: "Ice cream sales and drowning rates both rise in summer. Ice cream causes drowning."
Composition / Division
fallacia compositionis
Assuming what's true of the parts must be true of the whole (composition), or vice versa (division). The whole and its parts have different properties.
Example: "Every player on this team is excellent, therefore this is an excellent team."
Middle Ground
argumentum ad temperantiam
Assuming the truth must lie somewhere between two extremes. Sometimes it does. But if one position is simply wrong, the midpoint is also wrong.
Example: "Some say vaccines are safe, some say dangerous. The truth is probably somewhere in the middle."
Burden of Proof
onus probandi
Shifting the burden of proof to the person who doubts your claim. The person making a positive claim bears the burden of proving it. Absence of disproof is not proof.
Example: "Prove I'm wrong." — when they made the claim.
Fallacy Fallacy
argumentum ad logicam
Assuming that when an argument contains a fallacy, its conclusion must've been false as well. You can't just jump to the conclusion that easily.
Example: "This book contains a few fallacies, therefore everything in this book is false."
The Texas Sharpshooter
post designatio
When someone focuses on random clusters of data that supports their argument, while ignoring all the data that doesn't.
Example: "I chose 20 honor students and all of them are girls. Meaning girls are more smarter." - Ignores other data.
Fallacious Appeals
Appeal to Authority
argumentum ad verecundiam
Citing an authority as proof, without evaluating the actual argument. Experts can be wrong. An appeal to authority is evidence, not proof.
Example: "A professor said it, therefore it's true."
Appeal to Popularity
argumentum ad populum
Claiming something is true because many people believe it. Majorities have been spectacularly wrong throughout history.
Example: "Millions of people can't be wrong." (They can. They have been.)
Appeal to Emotion
argumentum ad passiones
Manipulating emotions instead of making a logical case. Feelings are real. They are not evidence of factual claims.
Example: "Think of the children!" — without addressing the actual argument.
Appeal to Nature
argumentum ad naturam
Claiming something is good because it's natural, or bad because it's unnatural. Nature contains both arsenic and blueberries.
Example: "It's completely natural, so it must be safe."
Appeal to Tradition
argumentum ad antiquitatem
Arguing something is better or right because it's older or traditional. Age does not validate. Many traditions were wrong.
Example: "We've always done it this way."
Appeal to Ignorance
argumentum ad ignorantiam
Claiming something must be true because it hasn't been proven false, or false because it hasn't been proven true. Lack of evidence is not evidence.
Example: "Nobody has proven it doesn't exist, therefore it exists."
Cognitive Biases
Confirmation Bias
myside bias
Seeking, interpreting, and remembering information in a way that confirms what you already believe. The most common bias. Everyone has it. The question is whether you fight it.
Example: Only reading news sources that agree with you, then concluding everyone agrees with you.
Dunning-Kruger Effect
illusory superiority
Those with limited knowledge in a domain overestimate their competence. The less you know, the less you know you don't know. Expertise breeds humility.
Example: Confidently explaining how vaccines work after reading three forum posts.
Survivorship Bias
survivorship bias
Focusing on things that "survived" a process and overlooking those that didn't, because the failures aren't visible. This distorts reality.
Example: "All those old buildings are so well-made!" — ignoring that the badly built ones collapsed centuries ago.
Availability Heuristic
availability bias
Overestimating the likelihood of events that come easily to mind, usually because they're recent, dramatic, or emotionally charged.
Example: Fearing plane crashes more than car crashes after seeing a news story, despite the statistics being reversed.
Sunk Cost Fallacy
concorde fallacy
Continuing with something because of previously invested resources (time, money, effort) rather than its future merit. Past costs are gone. Only future value matters.
Example: "I've argued this position for two hours — I can't back down now."
In-Group Bias
in-group favoritism
Favouring members of your own group and judging them by different standards than outsiders. Applies to nationality, ideology, fandom, and any other identity marker.
Example: Excusing the same behaviour in your group that you condemn in another.
Anchoring Bias
focalism
Over-relying on the first piece of information encountered when making decisions. The initial anchor shapes all subsequent judgements, even when it's arbitrary.
Example: A product "on sale" from $200 to $120 feels cheap — even if $120 was always the real price.
Bandwagon Effect
appeal to the masses
Adopting beliefs or behaviours because many others do, regardless of the underlying evidence. Popularity is not correctness.
Example: Changing your opinion the moment you notice it's the minority view in a thread.
Language & Rhetoric Tricks
Equivocation
fallacia aequivocationis
Using a word with two different meanings in the same argument as if they mean the same thing. Watch for terms that shift meaning mid-argument.
Example: "Laws of nature are laws. Laws require a lawmaker. Therefore nature has a lawmaker." (Two different meanings of "law.")
Loaded Question
complex question fallacy
Asking a question that presupposes something not established, forcing acceptance of an embedded assumption to answer at all.
Example: "Have you stopped being wrong about this?" (Either answer accepts that you were wrong.)
Moving the Goalposts
raising the bar
Changing the criteria for proof after evidence has been provided. Each time the standard is met, a new standard appears.
Example: "Show me one study." → "Show me ten studies." → "Show me a meta-analysis." → "Show me..."
Gish Gallop
spreading
Overwhelming an opponent with a large volume of weak arguments, so they can't address all of them. The quantity of claims is weaponized against quality of response.
Example: Posting 15 rapid-fire claims in one message, then saying "You haven't refuted any of them."
Social & Discussion Fallacies
Tu Quoque
appeal to hypocrisy
Deflecting criticism by pointing out that the critic also does the same thing. Hypocrisy is worth noting — but it doesn't make the original criticism wrong.
Example: "You can't criticize my argument — you've made the same mistake before."
Whataboutism
tu quoque variant
Deflecting criticism by pointing to someone else's unrelated fault. A political favorite. The existence of other problems doesn't negate the problem being discussed.
Example: "Why are you criticizing us? What about what they did?"
Genetic Fallacy
fallacia originis
Judging an argument as true or false based on its source or origin rather than its content. Where an idea comes from doesn't determine whether it's correct.
Example: "Of course you think that — you were raised to think that."
Tone Policing
tone argument
Dismissing an argument because of how it's delivered — too angry, too emotional, too blunt — without addressing whether the point itself is valid.
Example: "I might take you seriously if you weren't so worked up about it." (Addressing the tone, not the argument.)
Special Pleading
ad hoc rescue
Applying a special exemption to your own position when it would otherwise be refuted, without justifying why the exemption applies.
Example: "That rule applies to everyone except in this specific case, which is different, because... it just is."

The goal is not to "win." The goal is to be right.

Calling out a fallacy is not a personal attack — it's a request to rebuild the argument on solid ground. If someone drops a TSNCLF! on you, take it seriously. Find the fallacy. Fix the argument. Then come back stronger.
